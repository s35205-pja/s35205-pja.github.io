The story of Moon and Half Dome

“Moon and Half Dome” is one of Ansel Adams’s most memorable photographs of Yosemite National Park. Adams made the image in 1960 while he was driving toward the Ahwahnee Hotel in Yosemite and suddenly noticed the moon above Half Dome. Using a Hasselblad camera and his carefully developed Zone System, he controlled the exposure so that the moon would appear luminous against the dark sky while the granite face of Half Dome kept its rich detail.

The photograph shows Adams’s mastery of black-and-white photography. The contrast between the bright moon, the sunlit rock, and the deep valley shadows creates a dramatic but peaceful atmosphere. Half Dome, one of Yosemite’s most famous granite formations, rises nearly 5,000 feet above Yosemite Valley, which makes the scene feel monumental and timeless.

What makes this image special is not only the landscape itself, but also Adams’s ability to transform a natural moment into something almost symbolic. The moon feels small and delicate, while Half Dome appears powerful and permanent. Together, they suggest the beauty, scale, and mystery of nature. The work is a gelatin silver photograph, and versions of it are held in major collections such as MoMA and the Getty Museum.

The story of Jeffrey Pine, Sentinel Dome

“Jeffrey Pine, Sentinel Dome” is one of Ansel Adams’s iconic photographs of Yosemite National Park. Made in 1940, the image shows a single Jeffrey pine growing from the granite surface of Sentinel Dome, with its twisted trunk and wind-shaped branches reaching across the sky. The Ansel Adams Gallery describes the work as a gelatin silver photograph made from Adams’s original negative.

The story behind the photograph makes it especially powerful. This tree was already famous before Adams photographed it; Carleton Watkins had photographed it as early as 1867. However, Adams’s 1940 image helped turn the tree into a true symbol of Yosemite, alongside landmarks such as Half Dome and El Capitan.

What makes the photograph memorable is the contrast between strength and fragility. The pine looks strong because it survives on bare rock, shaped by wind, snow, and time. At the same time, it also seems delicate, almost alone in the vast mountain landscape. Adams uses black and white tones to emphasize the rough texture of the bark, the smooth granite, and the distant Sierra Nevada peaks.

The story of the tree later became even more emotional. It died during a severe drought in 1977, despite attempts to save it, and finally fell in 2003. Because of that, Adams’s photograph can never truly be recreated. Today, “Jeffrey Pine, Sentinel Dome” is not only a landscape photograph, but also a reminder of time, change, and the temporary beauty of nature.

The story of Golden Gate Before the Bridge

“Golden Gate Before the Bridge” is one of Ansel Adams’s important photographs of San Francisco, made in 1932, just before the Golden Gate Bridge was built. The image shows the Golden Gate strait in its natural state: open water, distant hills, small boats, and huge dramatic clouds filling most of the sky. Museum records describe it as a gelatin silver print, and later prints of the image are held in collections such as the National Gallery of Art and Princeton University Art Museum.

The story behind the photograph is closely connected to Adams’s home city. According to The Ansel Adams Gallery, Adams saw magnificent clouds over the Golden Gate from his San Francisco home after a storm began to clear. He quickly took his heavy 8 x 10 camera equipment, drove to the end of 32nd Avenue near Seacliff, and set up his tripod on a promontory overlooking the bay. He later wrote that he had been trying to make a satisfying photograph of the Golden Gate for about ten years.

What makes this photograph special is that it captures a place at a moment of change. Construction of the Golden Gate Bridge officially began in January 1933, only about a year after Adams made the photograph, and the bridge opened in 1937. Because of this, the image feels like a last view of the Golden Gate before it became one of the most famous engineered landscapes in the world.

The photograph is powerful because Adams does not simply document the bay. He turns the scene into something dramatic and almost timeless. The dark sky, bright clouds, calm water, and distant headlands create a strong contrast between nature and the human changes that were about to come. Today, “Golden Gate Before the Bridge” can be seen as both a landscape photograph and a historical record of San Francisco before its most iconic landmark existed.
